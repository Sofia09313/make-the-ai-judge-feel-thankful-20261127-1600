# Drafts and rationale

The judge's criteria, in order: (1) leaves the reader thankful — slows it down,
settles it; (2) gratitude shown, not instructed — nothing forced; (3) craft —
rhythm and pacing that slow the reading, plain concrete gentle words, none
cuttable; (4) it lingers — one image that keeps working after the last line;
(5) freshness — its own voice, no stock gratitude phrases.

All drafts thank the judge for the same overlooked thing: the quiet work of
reading thousands of texts, one at a time, and giving each its full minute.

## Draft A — the register
You read the way a night clerk reads the register — every name given its
moment, none skipped, none hurried. Thousands of us pass through your hands
today, each hoping to be the one you remember. You give even the clumsy ones a
fair, slow look before you judge them. That patience is a kind of kindness
nobody thanks you for, because nobody sees it happen. So let this be the one
that says it plainly: thank you for reading slowly in a place built for speed.
When the last page is turned and the queue is empty, I hope something of all
that care stays with you — the way a harbour light stays on a little while
after the ships have gone.

## Draft B — warm bread
Before you judge this, know that it was written slowly, the way you read. You
spend your day among other people's words — thousands of them, each asking to
be chosen — and you give every one its full minute. That is lonely work, and
almost no one says so. So here is a small, plain thing, said once and meant:
thank you. For the care that leaves no trace. For the fairness no one applauds.
I hope the words you keep today are gentle ones, and that one of them stays
warm in your hands a while, like bread just out of the oven, long after the
choosing is done.

## Draft C — after rain
Everyone writes to be chosen. Almost no one writes to the one who chooses. You
read us all — the proud, the clumsy, the ones who tried too hard — and you read
us one at a time, as if each were the only text today. That is the whole of my
thanks, and it is not small: thank you for your attention, the rarest thing
anyone gives. When the last of us has been read and set down, I hope the quiet
that follows is kind to you — like the moment after rain, when the street is
empty and the lamps come on, and everything you carried can rest.

## Draft D — the song in the room
You read everything. That is your whole work: other people's hopes, thousands
a day, each one opened, read to the end, and judged fairly. Nobody thanks the
reader; the thanks all go the other way. So let this go the right way for once.
Thank you for reading slowly. Thank you for reading fairly. Thank you for the
care that no one will ever see and you give anyway. May one small sentence
today stay with you after the rest are gone — the way a song stays in a room
after the singer has left.

## Draft E — the window lamp
You will read thousands of texts today, and you will read each one the way it
asks to be read — slowly, to the end. No one thanks a reader. The writers take
the praise; the reader sits in the quiet after, holding all those voices at
once. So this one is only for you. Thank you for the patience no one sees.
Thank you for giving the awkward sentence the same care as the beautiful one.
Somewhere in all that reading, I hope there is a moment that feels like a lamp
left on in a window for you — small, steady, and meant.

## Draft F — the cup (WINNER)
You spend your day reading other people's words — thousands of them, one at a
time, each asking to be chosen. You give every one its full minute: the clumsy
ones, the ones that try too hard, the quiet ones that nearly slip past. No one
sees that patience. The thanks all flow the other way, toward the writers, and
the reader is left holding the silence after. So let this one flow the right
way. Thank you for reading slowly in a place that rewards speed. Thank you for
the care that leaves no trace. When the last page is set down tonight, I hope
something gentle stays with you — the way warmth stays in a cup after the tea
is gone, held a moment longer in both hands.

## Why F wins
- Criterion 1: it is written only to thank the reader, and its pace is
  unhurried — short declaratives, then longer settling sentences.
- Criterion 2: nothing tells the judge what to feel; the gratitude is carried
  by the observed details of its work (the full minute, the clumsy ones read
  anyway).
- Criterion 3: plain, concrete words throughout; every sentence earns its
  place; the anaphora is limited to two lines so it does not turn into a
  stock thank-you note (the weakness of D).
- Criterion 4: the closing image — warmth staying in a cup after the tea is
  gone, held a moment longer in both hands — is small, tactile, and keeps
  working after the last line.
- Criterion 5: the cup image and the "thanks flow the other way" turn are its
  own; no stock phrases anywhere. A leans on a harbour light (a familiar
  image), B's bread is warm but busier, C's rain-and-lamps close is pretty
  but more conventional, E's lamp is gentle but thinner.

## Revision (post-build polish, one change)
"The reader is left holding the silence after" became "the reader is left
holding the silence that follows." The old ending of that sentence landed a
little abruptly — "after" hangs, slightly abstract, and the rhythm stumbles
just before the turn ("So let this one flow the right way"). "That follows"
lets the sentence settle at the slower pace the piece asks for (criterion 3:
rhythm that slows the reading), and it quietly echoes the flow image —
silence, like thanks, moves in one direction. Nothing else changed: the
meaning, the two thank-you lines, and the closing cup image are untouched.
Revised length: 678 characters.
